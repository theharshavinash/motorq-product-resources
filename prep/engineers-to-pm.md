# If you are an engineer considering product

Engineers are the largest single group applying to this track, so this is worth being direct about. Your background is an advantage: technical credibility, structured thinking, and comfort with data cover three of the six dimensions we score before you walk in. The objective of this page is to close the gaps that background does not cover, because those are what the interview actually tests.

The gaps are consistent, and they are not about intelligence:

- **Customer empathy.** Sitting with a user whose job is nothing like yours and taking their unglamorous workflow seriously.
- **Business framing.** Connecting a feature to what it changes for the customer and for us, not to how elegant it is.
- **Deciding without certainty.** Committing to a call when you do not have — and will not get — enough information.

## The four reframes that decide these interviews

Each of these is a habit engineers carry in that stops working the moment you are the one deciding what to build. Unlearning them is most of the transition.

1. **Interrogate the problem, not the solution.** The instinct is to optimise the thing in front of you. The job is to ask whether it should exist. Spend your first questions on who has this problem and how you know, before any discussion of what to build.

2. **Replace "it depends" with a committed call plus a stated confidence level.** "It depends" is where engineers hide, and it reads as an inability to decide. Say what you would do, then say how sure you are and what would change your mind. That is not less rigorous — it is more.

3. **Use feasibility to find the cheap 80%, not to explain why something can't be done.** You can see why an idea is hard. That is valuable only if you turn it into "here is the version we can ship this quarter that gets most of the value." Feasibility that only produces reasons to say no is a liability in a PM.

4. **Write for decisions, not documentation.** An engineer documents what is true. A PM writes to make someone decide. Every artifact should end somewhere — a recommendation, a cut, a number that should move — not merely be complete and correct.
